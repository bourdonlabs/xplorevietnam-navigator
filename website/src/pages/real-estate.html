Vietnam Guide: Real Estate

Real Estate and Rentals

Everything you need to know about moving to Vietnam.

Foreigners can buy homes in Vietnam, but with limits that differ from most Western countries. You can own an apartment or house in an approved residential project, for an initial term of 50 years, but you can’t own land, and only a share of each project can be sold to foreigners. Most expats rent, at least for their first year.

If you do buy, the process has its own structure and some important quirks. Working with the right professionals, particularly a real estate lawyer and a buyer’s agent, makes a significant difference to the experience.

The Property Purchase Process in Vietnam

From accepted offer to completed purchase, a resale apartment typically takes one to three months. New apartments bought from a developer follow the developer’s payment schedule, and the ownership certificate (the “pink book”) can take one to three years to be issued after handover.

Steps to purchase a home in Vietnam

  1. Define your search criteria. Choose your city and area, your budget, and whether you want a new apartment from a developer or a resale unit. Access to schools, healthcare, transport and your workplace are the most common anchors.
  2. Take an exploratory trip. Before making a significant investment, spend time in your shortlisted areas. Traffic, noise, flooding and building management vary a lot, even between neighbouring projects.
  3. Check that you can buy. Foreigners can only buy in projects approved for foreign ownership, and only while the foreign quota is not full. Confirm this before you go further.
  4. Engage a buyer’s agent. Listings are spread across many agents and websites, and the same unit is often advertised several times at different prices. A good agent knows which projects are open to foreigners.
  5. Search listings and view properties. The largest listing site is batdongsan.com.vn. Nhà Tốt (chotot.com), agents and Facebook groups are also widely used.
  6. Engage a real estate lawyer. A lawyer checks the title and the project’s legal status, reviews the contracts in Vietnamese and English, and protects you if something goes wrong.
  7. Agree the price in writing. Prices on resale units are usually negotiable. Agree who pays each tax and fee, as sellers often quote a price “net” of their own tax.
  8. Sign a deposit agreement. A deposit, often 5–10% for resale units, secures the property while due diligence is completed.
  9. Complete due diligence. Your lawyer checks the pink book, any mortgages or disputes on the property, the foreign quota and the project’s permits.
  10. Sign the sale and purchase contract. For a resale unit, the contract is signed at a notary office. For a new unit, it is signed with the developer.
  11. Pay through a Vietnamese bank. Payments must be made through a bank account in Vietnam, in Vietnamese dong. Keep every record of the money you bring in.
  12. Pay taxes and fees, and register the property. The registration fee is paid and your name is recorded on the pink book, or on a new pink book for a new unit.

What to Know About Vietnam’s Property Market

A few characteristics of the Vietnamese market are worth understanding before you start.

You own the home, not the land: All land in Vietnam belongs to the state. Foreigners can own the apartment or house, with land use rights attached, but not the land itself.

Ownership is limited to 50 years: Foreign ownership runs for 50 years from the date the pink book is issued, and can be extended once, for up to another 50 years.

Foreign quotas can fill up: Foreigners can own up to 30% of the units in an apartment building, and up to 250 houses in a ward. In popular projects, the foreign quota may already be used up.

Pink books can be delayed: On new projects, it can take years for pink books to be issued. Until then, you hold a contract, not a title.

Short-term rentals are restricted: Most apartment buildings don’t allow short stays such as Airbnb, so check the rules if you plan to rent your property out.

The Deposit and Sale Contract

For a resale property, the deposit agreement (hợp đồng đặt cọc) locks in the price and terms once your offer is accepted. The deposit is often 5–10% of the price, and the notarized sale and purchase contract follows after due diligence.

Unless the agreement says otherwise, Vietnam’s Civil Code works both ways. If you withdraw without cause, you lose the deposit. If the seller backs out, they must return your deposit and pay you the same amount again.

For new apartments, the law limits how much a developer can collect upfront, and at least 5% of the price is usually held back until your pink book is issued. Read the payment schedule carefully before you sign.

Property Inspections

Inspections are not a standard part of buying property in Vietnam, and the condition of a resale property is largely your responsibility to check.

We recommend an independent inspection for resale properties, especially older ones. Common issues include water leaks, damp, poor electrical work, cracked tiles and air conditioning problems.

For new apartments, inspect the unit carefully at handover and give the developer a written list of defects to fix.

Significant findings from an inspection may provide grounds for renegotiating the purchase price.

Closing Costs and Fees

Buying costs in Vietnam are relatively low. The main costs are:

  • VAT: 10% on new apartments bought from a developer, usually included in the price.
  • Maintenance fund: 2% of the price on new apartments, paid at handover.
  • Registration fee: 0.5% of the property value, paid by the buyer.
  • Notary fees: for resale properties, set on a sliding scale based on the price.
  • Legal fees: vary with the lawyer and the complexity of the purchase.

The seller normally pays personal income tax of 2% of the sale price, and the agent’s commission. In practice, many resale prices are quoted net of the seller’s costs, so confirm who pays what in writing.

Mortgages for Expats in Vietnam

Financing is available to some foreigners, but options are limited. Many foreign buyers pay cash or use a developer’s payment plan instead.

Eligibility: Banks generally lend only to foreigners who live and work in Vietnam, with a work permit, a temporary residence card and income paid into a Vietnamese bank account.

Down payment: Expect to put down 25–50% of the property value, depending on the bank and your profile.

Rates: Most loans offer a fixed rate for the first one to three years, then switch to a floating rate. Vietnamese mortgage rates are high compared with many Western countries.

Term: The loan term is often limited by the length of your residence card and work permit, as well as by your age.

Banks: A small number of banks, mainly foreign-owned ones, lend to foreigners. Speak to lenders early so you know your budget before you start viewing.

Transferring Money for Your Purchase

Money for your purchase must come into Vietnam through a bank and be paid in Vietnamese dong. Keep all transfer and conversion records: you will need them to prove where the money came from, and to send the proceeds back out of Vietnam if you sell. Using a specialist transfer service rather than a standard bank wire will usually get you a better exchange rate and lower fees. Check with your Vietnamese bank how large incoming transfers must be documented before you send the money.

Working with a Buyer’s Agent

Because there is no central listing system in Vietnam, the market is fragmented, and foreign buyers are at a disadvantage without local representation. A buyer’s agent helps you search efficiently, check which projects are open to foreigners, negotiate and avoid the pitfalls common to expat buyers.

There are two standard models:

Commission-based: The agent is paid from the seller’s or developer’s commission. This costs the buyer nothing extra, but the agent may be tied to particular projects.

Fixed fee: Some agents charge the buyer a fee instead. This can give you independent advice across a wider range of properties.

Renting in Vietnam

For most expats, renting is the right first step. It gives you time to learn your preferred area before committing to a purchase, and many expats rent for as long as they live here.

The rental market is active, with plenty of choice in Ho Chi Minh City, Hanoi and Da Nang. Prices in popular expat areas such as Thao Dien, District 1 and Tay Ho have risen in recent years, and good apartments at fair prices move quickly.

How Renting in Vietnam Works

The process is straightforward. Most leases are signed for 6 to 12 months, and shorter leases usually cost more per month. Contracts are often bilingual, in Vietnamese and English.

Landlords typically require a security deposit of one to two months’ rent, plus one to three months of rent in advance. Agent fees for rentals are usually paid by the landlord.

What Landlords Require

To rent in Vietnam, you will generally need:

  • A valid passport
  • A valid visa or temporary residence card
  • The deposit and first rent payment

By law, your landlord or building must declare your temporary residence to the police, usually within 12 hours of your arrival. Make sure this is done, as you may need it for your visa, residence card or bank account.

Where to Find Rentals

There is no central listing system in Vietnam. The main websites are batdongsan.com.vn and Nhà Tốt (chotot.com), and many rentals are found through agents and local Facebook groups. Photos and prices online are not always accurate, so always view a property, in person or by video, before paying.

Searching remotely from abroad is common and entirely workable. Most agents are used to organising video walkthroughs for clients who haven’t arrived yet.

Frequently Asked Questions: Property in Vietnam

Can I find a rental in Vietnam before I arrive?

Yes. Remote rental searches are common, and we run video walkthroughs of each property, covering the apartment, the building and the surrounding area, so you can decide with confidence from abroad.

How long are rental leases in Vietnam?

Most leases are for 6 to 12 months. Shorter stays are possible, but usually cost more per month.

Is the rental market competitive?

In popular expat areas, yes. Good apartments at fair prices move quickly, so start early and be ready to decide.

What if I don’t know where I want to live yet?

That’s a common starting point. Our rental search begins with a consultation about your lifestyle, priorities and budget, and we can help you choose the right area.

Can foreigners buy property in Vietnam?

Yes, with limits. Foreigners who have legally entered Vietnam can own apartments and houses in approved residential projects, for 50 years, renewable once, within the foreign quota. Foreigners can’t own land.

Do I need a residence card to buy property?

No. A valid passport and legal entry into Vietnam are generally enough to buy. A mortgage, however, usually requires a work permit and residence card.

How long does the purchase process take?

For a resale property, usually one to three months from an accepted offer. For a new apartment, the pink book can take one to three years after handover.

What is a pink book?

The pink book is the certificate of land use rights and home ownership. It is your legal title to the property.

What taxes do I pay when buying property in Vietnam?

The buyer usually pays a 0.5% registration fee, plus 10% VAT and a 2% maintenance fund on new apartments. The seller usually pays 2% personal income tax on the sale price.

Can I get a mortgage as an expat in Vietnam?

Sometimes. A few banks lend to foreigners who work in Vietnam, usually with a down payment of 25–50%. Many foreign buyers pay cash instead.

Is a property inspection legally required?

No. We still recommend one for resale properties, and a careful defect check at handover for new apartments.

Do I need a lawyer?

Yes. A lawyer is essential for a purchase in Vietnam, and we recommend a legal review of any lease before signing.

Can I rent out the property I buy?

Yes, on longer leases, as long as you register and pay tax on the rental income. Most apartment buildings don’t allow short-term rentals such as Airbnb.

What happens if the seller backs out after I pay a deposit?

Unless your agreement says otherwise, the seller must return your deposit and pay you the same amount again.
