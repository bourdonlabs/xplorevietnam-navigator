Vietnam Guide: Hanoi

Living in Hanoi as an Expat

Everything you need to know about moving to Hanoi.

Hanoi is Vietnam’s capital and its cultural heart: a thousand-year-old city of lakes, temples, tree-lined boulevards, French colonial buildings and some of the best street food in Asia. It is busy and noisy, with motorbikes on the road from 6am, but it is also more traditional and a little slower than Ho Chi Minh City, and many people who come “just for a year” end up staying much longer. This guide covers where to live, what it costs, how to get around, work, healthcare, schools, money and daily life.

Since July 2025, Vietnam has removed the district level of government, so official Hanoi addresses now use wards and communes. Everyone still uses the old district names, such as Tay Ho and Ba Dinh, and so do we.

Why Expats Choose Hanoi

Hanoi suits people who want a real city with character, history and seasons, at a lower cost than Ho Chi Minh City.

  • Culture: the Old Quarter, Hoan Kiem Lake, the Temple of Literature, museums, festivals and a strong sense of tradition
  • Food and drink: phở, bún chả, egg coffee and the city’s famous bia hơi, fresh draught beer served on tiny plastic stools
  • Community: a friendly, well-connected expat community, with sports teams, classes and events every week
  • Work: the capital is home to government, embassies, NGOs and many international companies, and it is one of the biggest markets in Asia for English teachers
  • Four seasons: a hot summer, a cool winter and a beautiful autumn, unlike the year-round heat of the south

The downsides are real. Air pollution is the number one complaint, especially in winter, and the traffic, noise and crowds take some getting used to. Most people find the kindness of the people and the energy of the city more than make up for it.

Best Neighborhoods for Expats in Hanoi

Hanoi is a safe city with no real no-go areas, so you can live almost anywhere. Most foreigners, though, choose one of a handful of neighborhoods, depending on work, school and how local they want life to be.

Tay Ho (West Lake)

Tay Ho is where most expats live, set around West Lake, the city’s largest lake. It has international restaurants, cafés, bars, gyms, clinics and shops, English is widely spoken, and the lakeside roads are good for walking, running and cycling. It is the most expensive area, but it is beautiful, easy to settle into and close to several international schools, which makes it the first choice for many families and long-term residents.

Ba Dinh

Ba Dinh sits between Tay Ho and the Old Quarter. It is the political center of Vietnam, with government buildings, embassies and wide, green streets, as well as Truc Bach Lake. It has plenty of international businesses, but many more Vietnamese residents than Tay Ho, so it suits people who want home comforts while still feeling immersed in local life. It is generally cheaper than Tay Ho.

Hoan Kiem (Old Quarter)

Hoan Kiem is the historic heart of Hanoi, around Hoan Kiem Lake and the narrow streets of the Old Quarter, and the city’s tourist and backpacker hub. It is full of food, culture and character, but it is crowded and noisy day and night. It is a great base for your first weeks while you look for somewhere to live, less so for family life.

Dong Da

Dong Da is next to Ba Dinh, and the two blend into each other. It is almost entirely Vietnamese, with many schools, universities and language centers, and it is one of the more affordable central areas. It suits people who want a local way of life and don’t need an expat scene on the doorstep.

Cau Giay

Cau Giay is a modern district in the west of the city, with high-rise apartments, offices and universities. It offers newer apartments at better prices than Tay Ho, and suits professionals who work nearby.

Ciputra, Long Bien and the large developments

Ciputra, north-west of West Lake, is a large gated community with villas, apartments, shops and UNIS, the United Nations International School. The Vinhomes developments in Long Bien, across the Red River, and complexes such as Times City and Royal City, which have their own Vinschool campuses, offer a similar self-contained lifestyle. Many families choose these areas to be close to school and to have green space and security, at the cost of being further from the center.

Renting an Apartment in Hanoi

Finding a place in Hanoi is quick. Many people find a room or apartment within a few days of arriving. The main places to look are local Facebook groups, such as the Hanoi Massive groups and rental groups, and listing sites such as The New Hanoian. Agents respond fast and are usually paid by the landlord.

What rent costs

  • Tay Ho: around $700–$1,300 a month for a modern one-bedroom apartment
  • Cau Giay: around $500–$900 a month for a one-bedroom apartment
  • Ba Dinh and Dong Da: usually a step down from Tay Ho
  • Shared houses and local-style apartments: from around VND 7 million (about $270) a month

Many expats share a house with roommates to cut costs. Landlords usually ask for a deposit of one or two months’ rent, plus one to three months paid in advance, and rent is often negotiable on longer leases.

Before you sign

  • Visit in person, at different times of day, before paying anything
  • Check the air conditioning has a heating mode, as Hanoi homes have no central heating and winters are cold and damp
  • Look for damp and mould, which are common in the humid spring
  • Make sure the contract says who pays for utilities, internet and repairs, and how electricity is charged
  • Check that the landlord will register your temporary residence with the local police, which is required for every foreigner and needed for work permit and residence card applications

Cost of Living in Hanoi

Hanoi is very affordable by Western standards and generally a little cheaper than Ho Chi Minh City. Rent is the biggest cost. Local food, transport and services are cheap.

Everyday prices

  • Local meal, such as bún chả, phở or cơm bình dân: VND 40,000–100,000 ($1.50–$4)
  • Groceries if you cook at home: around VND 2–4 million ($75–$155) a month per person
  • GrabBike ride: VND 13,000–60,000 ($0.50–$2.30) depending on distance
  • GrabCar ride: VND 40,000–200,000 ($1.50–$7.70)
  • Motorbike rental: around VND 800,000–1,200,000 ($30–$46) a month for a basic bike
  • Phone plan with data: around VND 100,000 ($4) every few weeks

Local markets and roadside sellers are the cheapest places for fresh fruit and vegetables. Imported food and Western brands cost much more. Electricity bills rise in summer and winter, when the air conditioning runs for cooling or heating.

Getting Around Hanoi

Ride-hailing and taxis

Grab is the easiest way to get around, with motorbike and car options and the price shown before you book. Be and Xanh SM, an all-electric service, are popular alternatives. If you take a regular taxi, use a reputable company and make sure the meter is running.

Metro and buses

Hanoi has two metro lines. Line 2A runs from Cat Linh to Ha Dong in the south-west, and the elevated section of Line 3 runs from Nhon to Cau Giay in the west, with more lines under construction. The city bus network is large and very cheap, but slower and harder to use without Vietnamese.

Riding a motorbike

Many Hanoi residents get around by motorbike, and it is the most flexible way to live in the city. Before you ride, get the paperwork right:

  • Vietnam requires a licence for any motorbike of 50cc or more. If you live here, you need a Vietnamese licence, converted from your home licence at the Traffic Police Department with your residence permit and a notarized translation of your licence.
  • Helmets are compulsory for the rider and passenger, so buy a good-quality one.
  • The drink-driving limit is zero, with no allowance at all.
  • Riding without the right licence can invalidate your insurance if you have an accident.

If you buy a bike, make sure it comes with its blue registration card, and test ride it first. Take lessons if you have never ridden before, and practice on quiet streets before tackling the Old Quarter at rush hour.

Hanoi has also announced plans to phase out petrol motorbikes in the city center, starting inside Ring Road 1 from July 2026, so check the current rules before you buy a petrol bike. An electric bike may be the better choice.

Working in Hanoi

Teaching English

Teaching English is the most common job for foreigners in Hanoi, in language centers, public schools, international schools and universities. The biggest hiring periods are August, before the school year starts, and around March. Avoid arriving just before Tet, the Lunar New Year, when schools, businesses and government offices close for one to three weeks.

To work legally you need a work permit, which your employer applies for. For teaching, employers usually ask for:

  • A university degree, legalized for use in Vietnam
  • A TEFL, TESOL or CELTA certificate, legalized the same way, unless you have a teaching degree such as a PGCE
  • A health check at an approved hospital
  • A police check, and your temporary residence registration

Working on a tourist visa or e-visa is not legal, and it puts both you and your employer at risk of fines and deportation. Get your work permit and the right visa or residence card in place, and look into an employer carefully before you sign, ideally by talking to teachers who work there.

Other work

As the capital, Hanoi has embassies, NGOs, development agencies and many international companies, but most of these roles are filled by people hired from abroad or transferred in. Remote work is the other common route, and Hanoi has a large community of remote workers. Remote workers still need a visa that matches how long they stay and what they do.

Healthcare and Safety in Hanoi

Hospitals and clinics

Hanoi has good private and international healthcare. Most expats use international clinics and hospitals such as Family Medical Practice in Ba Dinh, the Hanoi French Hospital in Dong Da and Vinmec Times City, where doctors speak English and standards are international. Public hospitals are cheaper but crowded, with little English.

Pharmacies are everywhere and many medicines are sold without a prescription. Don’t take antibiotics or strong medicines without a doctor’s advice, and use a translation app if the pharmacist doesn’t speak English.

Health insurance

Routine care is cheap, but a serious accident or illness is not, and hospitals may ask for proof of insurance or payment before treatment. Most employers provide some insurance, and many expats add private international cover.

Emergencies

Dial 115 for an ambulance, 113 for police and 114 for fire. Save the emergency number of your nearest international hospital, and keep your insurance details and a copy of your passport with you.

Air pollution

Hanoi’s air is often poor, especially from late autumn to early spring. Check the air quality daily on an app such as IQAir, wear a good mask on the motorbike, run an air purifier at home, and plan outdoor exercise for the cleaner days.

Staying safe

Hanoi is very safe, and violent crime is rare. Watch out for bag and phone snatching in busy areas, and take care on the roads, which are the biggest everyday risk. When crossing the street, walk slowly and steadily and let the traffic flow around you.

International Schools in Hanoi

Hanoi has the second-largest choice of international schools in Vietnam, with British, American, IB and other curricula. Many are around Tay Ho and Ciputra, where UNIS is based, and in Long Bien, and the Vinschool system has campuses in developments such as Times City and Royal City. Fees at international schools in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City typically range from around $11,000 to $38,000 a year, depending on the school and grade.

Choose the school first and then the neighborhood. A short school run makes family life much easier.

Banking, Money and Phones

Opening a bank account

A local bank account makes it easy to pay rent, receive a salary and use payment apps. Banks generally ask for your passport, a valid visa or residence card and proof of address, and some also ask for a labor contract. HSBC and Standard Chartered offer good English-language service and mobile apps, and Techcombank and Vietcombank are widely used local banks. Sending money abroad from a Vietnamese account requires proof of where it came from, such as your labor contract and tax records, so keep your paperwork in order.

Cash and payments

Cash is still common, but QR payments and apps such as MoMo and ZaloPay are used almost everywhere. Change money at banks or licensed exchange counters.

Phones and power

Buy a local SIM when you arrive. Viettel has the best coverage, and Vinaphone and Mobifone are the main alternatives. You need your passport to register it. Vietnam uses 220V electricity, with sockets that take two-pin plugs.

Food, Coffee and Community

Food and drink

Hanoi is one of the great food cities of Asia. Try phở for breakfast, bún chả at lunch, chả cá, bánh cuốn and the endless street stalls, and join the locals on plastic stools at a bia hơi, where a glass of fresh beer costs very little and you will hear the famous toast, “Một, hai, ba, dzô!”

Coffee

Hanoi takes coffee seriously. Egg coffee was invented here, and there are also coconut coffee, salt coffee and strong traditional drip coffee. Cafés range from tiny street-side spots to quiet rooftops that make good remote offices.

Meeting people

The expat community is one of Hanoi’s biggest strengths. Join a sports team, a class or a language exchange, and follow Facebook groups such as the Hanoi Massive groups and, for women, Hanoi Beautiful, to see what is on. Vietnamese people are welcoming and curious about foreigners, and if you make local friends you will often be invited to join family celebrations and festivals such as Tet.

A little Vietnamese goes a long way

More and more people in Hanoi speak English, but learning the basics, such as numbers, greetings and how to order food and coffee, makes daily life easier and earns a lot of goodwill:

  • Xin chào: Hello
  • Cảm ơn: Thank you
  • Bao nhiêu tiền?: How much?

Hanoi’s accent is different from the south’s, and the tones take practice, but you will pick things up faster than you expect. Note that the city pauses for a nap from about 12 to 1pm, so many small businesses and offices go quiet at lunchtime.

Pets

Cats are easy to keep in Hanoi. Dogs are harder: green space is limited, the streets are crowded and dog theft does happen, so keep dogs on a leash and never leave them unattended outside. Families with dogs often choose Tay Ho or the larger developments with parks.

Climate in Hanoi

Unlike the south, Hanoi has four seasons:

  • Summer (about May to September): hot and humid, often well over 35°C, with heavy rain and occasional storms
  • Autumn (about October to November): the best time of year, dry, sunny and mild
  • Winter (about December to February): cold and damp, sometimes close to 10°C, and homes have no central heating
  • Spring (about March to April): mild, misty and very humid

Don’t move with only summer clothes. You will need a warm coat, and a scarf and gloves if you ride a motorbike in winter.

Weekend Escapes from Hanoi

Northern Vietnam has some of the country’s most spectacular landscapes, and much of it is within a few hours of the city.

Ninh Binh

About two hours south, Ninh Binh has limestone mountains, rice fields, rivers and caves, and is an easy weekend trip.

Ha Long Bay

The famous limestone islands of Ha Long Bay, a UNESCO World Heritage site, are a few hours east by road, with overnight cruises and quieter alternatives such as Lan Ha Bay.

Sa Pa and the mountains

Sa Pa, in the far north-west, offers rice terraces, hill tribe villages and trekking, and is reachable by bus or overnight train. Mai Chau and Ba Vi National Park are closer options for a quick escape.

Further afield

Noi Bai International Airport has frequent, cheap flights to Da Nang, Ho Chi Minh City, Phu Quoc and across Asia.

Final Thoughts on Living in Hanoi

Hanoi is not the easiest city to arrive in: the traffic, noise, pollution and winter all take adjustment. But it rewards people who give it time, with a rich culture, great food, low costs and one of the friendliest expat communities in Asia.

Get the basics right before you go: the right visa and, if you are working, a work permit through a reputable employer; health insurance; a warm coat; and a few weeks in short-term accommodation while you choose your neighborhood.

Frequently Asked Questions: Living in Hanoi

Where do most expats live in Hanoi?

Most live in Tay Ho, around West Lake. Ba Dinh is the next most popular, with a more local feel, and families often choose Ciputra, Long Bien or the large developments near international schools.

How much does it cost to live in Hanoi?

A couple living comfortably in a two-bedroom apartment in Tay Ho spends around $2,700 a month. Singles in shared houses or more local neighborhoods can live on much less.

Is Hanoi cheaper than Ho Chi Minh City?

Generally yes, a little, especially for rent outside Tay Ho. Day-to-day costs such as food and transport are similar.

Can I teach English in Hanoi without a work permit?

Not legally. You need a work permit, which your employer applies for, usually with a legalized degree and a TEFL or similar certificate. Working without one risks fines and deportation.

Do I need a licence to ride a motorbike in Hanoi?

Yes. Residents need a Vietnamese licence for any bike of 50cc or more, and riding without one can invalidate your insurance.

How bad is the air pollution in Hanoi?

It is often poor, especially in winter. Most residents check the air quality daily, wear a mask on the road and use an air purifier at home.

Does it get cold in Hanoi?

Yes. Winter, from about December to February, is cold and damp, and homes have no central heating, so bring warm clothes and choose an apartment with heating.
