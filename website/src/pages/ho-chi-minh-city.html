Vietnam Guide: Ho Chi Minh City

Living in Ho Chi Minh City as an Expat

Everything you need to know about moving to Ho Chi Minh City.

Ho Chi Minh City, still called Saigon by almost everyone who lives there, is Vietnam’s biggest city and its commercial capital. It is loud, fast and crowded, and it is also one of the easiest places in Asia to build a comfortable life on a reasonable budget. This guide covers what you need to decide before you move: where to live, what it costs, how to get around, healthcare, schools, money and daily life.

The city grew a lot on paper in July 2025, when it merged with Binh Duong and Ba Ria-Vung Tau provinces. The expanded city covers about 6,772 km² and has around 14 million people, with roughly 9.9 million in the core urban area. The same reform removed the district level of government, so official addresses now use wards, but everyone still uses the old district names, and so do we.

Why Expats Choose Ho Chi Minh City

Ho Chi Minh City is where most of Vietnam’s business happens, so it has the widest choice of jobs, housing, international schools and private hospitals in the country. It also has the largest expat community, which makes the first few months much easier.

  • Opportunity: strong demand for foreign professionals in education, finance, technology, manufacturing and professional services, and the most common first job for newcomers, teaching English
  • Cost: a comfortable lifestyle costs far less than in most Western cities, especially if you eat locally
  • Food and nightlife: street food on every corner, international restaurants, rooftop bars, craft breweries and a café culture that runs from early morning to late at night
  • Infrastructure: the country’s busiest international airport, Tan Son Nhat, a new metro line and a large choice of modern apartment buildings

The trade-offs are real too. Traffic is heavy, the air quality is poor on many days, the heat is constant and some streets flood in the rainy season. People who love the city tend to love its energy. People who don’t usually move on to Da Nang or Hoi An for a slower pace.

Best Neighborhoods for Expats in Ho Chi Minh City

Where you live will shape your experience of the city more than any other decision. Commute times, noise, flooding and access to schools all vary a lot from one area to the next, so pick your neighborhood around where you will work or where your children will go to school, not around rent alone.

District 1

District 1 is the center of the city, with the main business district, banks, consulates, landmark hotels, rooftop bars and many of the best restaurants. Ben Thanh Market, the War Remnants Museum and Notre Dame Cathedral are all here, and Bui Vien walking street is the backpacker nightlife strip. It is the most convenient place to live, but apartments are smaller, traffic never stops and noise is a real issue. It suits single professionals and couples who want to walk or take short rides to work and to everything else.

Tips: choose a building with good sound insulation, visit at night before you sign, and expect higher service charges and parking fees.

Thao Dien

Thao Dien, in the former District 2, is the heart of the expat community. It has tree-lined streets, villas, cafés, co-working spaces, international restaurants and grocery stores, and several of the city’s best-known international schools, including ISHCMC, BIS, AIS and EIS. It is quieter and greener than the center and very popular with families. Many newcomers start in one of the large apartment complexes, such as Masteri, and Metro Line 1 now connects the area to District 1.

It is one of the more expensive neighborhoods and something of an expat bubble. Some streets flood in the rainy season, so ask landlords directly about flooding, check basement parking, and test your commute at rush hour before you commit.

Thu Thiem

Thu Thiem is the new urban area directly across the river from District 1, with modern high-rise projects, wide roads and river views. It is still being built out, so services are thinner than in Thao Dien, but it has some of the newest apartments in the city and is only minutes from the center.

Binh Thanh

Binh Thanh sits between District 1 and Thao Dien and is the value choice for many newcomers. It is a large, mostly local district, but it also includes Vinhomes Central Park and Landmark 81, one of the tallest buildings in Southeast Asia, with a riverside park, a large mall and high-end apartments. Quality varies a lot from street to street, so inspect the neighborhood as carefully as the apartment, and choose a building with proper security.

District 3

District 3 borders District 1 and offers central living without the same intensity. It has tree-lined streets, French colonial villas, local markets, offices and many of the city’s best cafés, as well as Tan Dinh Church, the famous pink church. There are fewer large residential towers, so housing is more often renovated older buildings and serviced apartments. Check the building’s age, electrics and internet, and look for streets set back from the main roads.

District 7 (Phu My Hung)

Phu My Hung, in District 7, is a planned area in the south of the city, with wide streets, pavements, parks, shopping malls, international hospitals and schools such as Saigon South International School. It is clean, calm and orderly, and popular with families, particularly from Korea and Japan. The main drawback is distance: getting to District 1 or District 3 can take 30 to 45 minutes in traffic, so budget for the time and the extra ride costs.

District 4

District 4 sits between District 1 and District 7 and is one of the most affordable central areas. It is mostly local, with great street food, and it is common to find shared houses here, which keeps costs even lower. It suits people who want a local way of life while staying close to the center.

Phu Nhuan

Phu Nhuan is a compact, central neighborhood close to the airport, with a local feel, excellent street food and lower rents than the expat areas.

Renting an Apartment in Ho Chi Minh City

Finding an apartment is easy. Local Facebook rental groups and property websites are full of listings, and if you comment on a post, agents will message you within minutes. Agents are usually paid by the landlord, so using one normally costs you nothing extra.

Deposits and leases

Landlords usually ask for a deposit of one or two months’ rent, plus rent paid one to three months in advance. Leases are typically six to twelve months, and rents are often negotiable on longer leases. Never pay a deposit before you, or someone you trust, has seen the apartment in person.

Before you sign

  • Visit at different times of day to check noise and traffic
  • Look for nearby construction, which is common and can last for years
  • Ask whether the street or building floods in the rainy season
  • Make sure the contract says who pays for utilities, internet and repairs, and how electricity is charged
  • Check that the landlord will register your temporary residence with the local police, which is required for every foreigner

Modern buildings usually include 24-hour security, a gym, a pool and parking. Newer does not always mean better located, though, so weigh the building against the commute.

Cost of Living in Ho Chi Minh City

Ho Chi Minh City is the most expensive city in Vietnam, but it is still very affordable by Western standards. Housing is the biggest cost. Food, transport and services are cheap, as long as you live partly like a local.

Typical monthly budgets

  • Single person, comfortable lifestyle: around $1,500–$1,900 a month
  • Couple without children: around $2,300–$2,900 a month
  • Families: much more once international school fees are included

These assume modern housing, eating out regularly, ride-hailing and basic leisure. You can live for much less in local-style housing, or spend a lot more in premium buildings.

Everyday prices

  • Local meal: $2–$4
  • Mid-range restaurant meal: $10–$20
  • Vietnamese coffee: around $1, more in specialty cafés
  • Utilities (electricity, water, rubbish): $50–$90 a month, more if you run air conditioning all night
  • Gym membership: $40–$75 a month
  • Transport for a daily commuter: $40–$90 a month

Imported groceries can cost two to three times what you would pay at home, so most expats buy fresh produce locally and imported items selectively. Electricity is the bill that surprises people: from March to May, running the air conditioning every night can double it.

Costs people forget

Visa renewals, annual health insurance premiums, travel over Tet and public holidays, setting up a home (furniture, kitchenware, appliances) and higher power bills in the hot season don’t show up every month, but they add up. Setting aside 5–10% of your monthly income as a buffer makes them much easier to absorb.

Getting Around Ho Chi Minh City

The city has millions of motorbikes and traffic can feel chaotic at first, but getting around is cheap and quickly becomes routine.

Ride-hailing apps

Most expats rely on ride-hailing apps. Grab is the biggest, and Be and Xanh SM, an all-electric service, are popular alternatives. All show the price before you book and accept cashless payment. Motorbike rides are faster in traffic. Car rides are more comfortable in the heat and the rain, and a good way to start while you get used to the roads.

Taxis

Vinasun and Mai Linh are the reputable taxi companies. Make sure the meter is running, avoid unbranded taxis and keep small notes for payment. Taxis are handy at the airport, at hotels and late at night.

Metro and buses

Metro Line 1, the city’s first, opened in December 2024 and runs from Ben Thanh in District 1 to Suoi Tien, passing Thao Dien and An Phu on the way. It is fast, air-conditioned and cheap, and more lines are planned. City buses cost around $0.25 a trip and the network is large, but they are slow and hard to use without Vietnamese, so most expats only use them occasionally.

Riding a motorbike

Renting a motorbike costs around $60–$80 a month long term, and buying one costs roughly $750–$1,500 depending on the model. If you buy, make sure it comes with its blue registration card and test ride it first. Before you ride, get a valid Vietnamese motorbike licence, check that your insurance explicitly covers riding, and buy a good-quality helmet. Riding without a licence can void your insurance completely, and treatment after an accident is expensive without cover. Practice on quiet streets in Thao Dien or District 7 before riding in the center.

Rush hour

Traffic peaks from about 7 to 10am and 5 to 8pm, when even short trips take much longer. Time, not money, is the real cost of transport here, which is why living close to work or school matters so much.

Healthcare and Safety in Ho Chi Minh City

Hospitals and clinics

Ho Chi Minh City has the best healthcare in the south of Vietnam. Public hospitals are cheap but crowded, with little English, so most expats use private and international facilities such as FV Hospital, Vinmec Central Park, Family Medical Practice and International SOS. They offer English-speaking doctors, short waits and international standards. A consultation typically costs $50–$100 before tests and medication, and specialist or hospital care gets expensive quickly, so good health insurance matters.

Pharmacies are everywhere and many medicines are sold without a prescription. Don’t take antibiotics without medical advice, and use reputable pharmacies, ideally near an international clinic.

Emergencies

Dial 115 for an ambulance, 113 for police and 114 for fire. Ambulances can be slow in traffic, so save the emergency number of your nearest international hospital. Hospitals often ask for proof of insurance or payment before treatment, so keep your insurance details on your phone and in your wallet, along with a copy of your passport.

Staying safe

Violent crime is rare, but phone and bag snatching from passing motorbikes does happen. Use a phone lanyard or wrist strap, don’t hold your phone out at the roadside, and wear bags across your body, especially in the weeks before Tet. Most apartment buildings have security staff and controlled access.

Road safety is the bigger risk. When you cross the street, walk slowly and steadily and let traffic flow around you rather than stopping suddenly. Many residents wear a mask on the road, both for the pollution and for the strong sun.

International Schools in Ho Chi Minh City

Ho Chi Minh City has the largest choice of international schools in Vietnam, with British, American, IB, Australian, Canadian and French curricula. Most are in Thao Dien and District 7, with others around the center. Fees typically range from around $11,000 to $38,000 a year, depending on the school and grade, before uniforms, transport and activities, so most families budget for school fees annually rather than monthly.

Choose the school before the apartment. A short school run makes a big difference to family life in this traffic.

Banking and Money in Ho Chi Minh City

Cash and the dong

Cash is still widely used, although QR payments are now everywhere. The Vietnamese dong has large numbers: VND 10,000 is about $0.40, VND 100,000 about $4 and VND 500,000 about $20. Some notes look alike, particularly the VND 20,000 and VND 500,000, so keep your notes sorted by value and carry smaller ones, as street vendors and taxis often can’t change a VND 500,000 note. Change money at banks or licensed exchange counters, never on the street.

Opening a bank account

You will need a local bank account to pay rent, receive a salary and use payment apps. Banks generally ask for your passport, a valid visa or residence card and proof of a local address or employment. HSBC and Standard Chartered offer good English-language service, while local banks such as Vietcombank and Techcombank are well connected to local payment systems. Ask about international transfer fees and minimum balances before you choose.

Food, Coffee and Community

Food

Food is one of the best reasons to live here. You will find phở and cơm tấm on the pavement, push carts selling fruit with chilli salt, and restaurants from almost every country. GrabFood and ShopeeFood deliver almost anything. Start with busy stalls, where turnover keeps food fresh, introduce local dishes gradually, and use bottled water for brushing your teeth and cooking.

Coffee

Vietnam is one of the world’s largest coffee producers, and Saigon runs on it. Try cà phê sữa đá, iced coffee with condensed milk, then work through salt coffee, coconut coffee and the rest. It is strong, so go slowly. Many cafés have reliable Wi-Fi and are used as offices by remote workers.

Meeting people

The city has a large and active expat community, but friendships still take effort. Join Facebook groups such as Expats in Ho Chi Minh City, and Fexpats Ho Chi Minh for women, go to networking events, or join a sports club, fitness class, language exchange or volunteering group. Making Vietnamese friends makes the experience much richer.

A little Vietnamese goes a long way

English is common in business and the expat areas, but a few words of Vietnamese earn a lot of goodwill:

  • Xin chào: Hello
  • Cảm ơn: Thank you
  • Bao nhiêu tiền?: How much?

Politeness and patience matter. Greet staff, don’t raise your voice and say thank you, and doors open.

Climate in Ho Chi Minh City

Ho Chi Minh City is hot all year, with two seasons. The dry season runs from about December to April, and March to May is the hottest stretch. The rainy season runs from about May to November, with short, heavy downpours most afternoons that can flood some streets for an hour or two. It rarely rains all day.

Plan around the afternoon rain in the wet season, keep a rain poncho in your bag if you ride, and avoid your first motorbike ride in a storm. Sunscreen and a hat are needed all year.

Weekend Escapes from Ho Chi Minh City

Regular breaks from the city help, especially in the hot season, and there is a lot within reach.

Vung Tau

Now part of Ho Chi Minh City itself, the coastal town of Vung Tau is about two hours by road, or less by fast ferry. It is the classic weekend escape, with beaches, seafood and a long-established community of expats and retirees.

The Mekong Delta and Can Tho

The Mekong Delta starts just south of the city, with rivers, orchards and village life. Can Tho, the delta’s largest city, is about three to four hours away by road and is famous for its floating markets.

Further afield

Tan Son Nhat airport has cheap, frequent flights to Hanoi, Da Nang, Phu Quoc and Da Lat, and across Southeast Asia, so a long weekend away is easy to arrange.

Final Thoughts on Living in Ho Chi Minh City

Ho Chi Minh City rewards people who come prepared. Sort out your visa and work status first, choose your neighborhood around your work or your children’s school, and give yourself a few weeks in short-term accommodation before you sign a lease. Budget for the costs that don’t come every month, get proper health insurance, and take the traffic seriously.

Get those things right and the city gives back a lot: work opportunities, great food, a big international community and a quality of life that is hard to match for the price.

Frequently Asked Questions: Living in Ho Chi Minh City

What is the population of Ho Chi Minh City?

Around 14 million since the July 2025 merger with Binh Duong and Ba Ria-Vung Tau, making it Vietnam’s largest city by far. The core urban area has roughly 9.9 million people.

How much does it cost to live in Ho Chi Minh City?

A single person typically needs around $1,500–$1,900 a month for a comfortable lifestyle with modern housing, eating out and ride-hailing, and a couple around $2,300–$2,900. Families should add international school fees on top.

Where do most expats live in Ho Chi Minh City?

District 1 for professionals who want to be central, Thao Dien and District 7 for families, District 3 for a balanced central life, and Binh Thanh or District 4 for better value.

Is Ho Chi Minh City safe for expats?

Yes. Violent crime is rare. The main risks are phone and bag snatching and road safety, so secure your valuables, and if you ride a motorbike, get a licence, a good helmet and insurance that covers riding.

Is Ho Chi Minh City good for families?

Yes. It has the widest choice of international schools and hospitals in Vietnam. Most families settle in Thao Dien or District 7, close to school, and the biggest cost to plan for is school fees.

Do I need a car in Ho Chi Minh City?

No. Ride-hailing apps are cheap and available everywhere, and Metro Line 1 now links the center to Thao Dien. Owning a car is expensive and parking is difficult, so very few expats do.

When is the rainy season in Ho Chi Minh City?

From about May to November, with heavy afternoon downpours rather than all-day rain. The dry season runs from about December to April.
